Act 0 · Framing0:02 – 0:05

The choice was never "relational or document"

For twenty years we've argued models like they were teams to join. They were never teams. They are projections of the same truth, each shaped by what the engine of the day could afford. Change the engine, and the argument changes.

The old frame

Pick a database per workload

Normalize here, denormalize there, buy a purpose-built store for each access pattern, and glue them together. Every pattern in the catalog is a coping strategy for one engine's limits — and every glue joint is an integration tax and a consistency gap.

The frame for today

Model once, project many

One canonical form. Many projected shapes — document, graph, vector, relational — served from the same rows, in one transaction, under one optimizer. The pattern doesn't disappear; the cost of choosing it does.

  1. Why converged, why now — the timeline that produced the patterns, and Unified Model Theory.
  2. The physics — three knobs, write amplification, and the breakpoint that decides every pattern.
  3. The pattern walk — six industries, six hard edges, and how converged softens each one.
  4. How you know — the oracle-ai-dev bake-off: reason, then measure where the needle flips.

This is a modeling session, not a product pitch. The rule we're testing all day: the workload chooses the shape — you measure, you don't guess.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:05 – 0:09

Data pressure through the ages

Every era's dominant model was the right bet for that era's bottleneck. The patterns we argue about weren't wrong — they were bets placed against a constraint that has since moved.

The document patterns you use every day were invented in the third box — bets against a distributed engine that couldn't join, couldn't transact across shards, and re-serialized a whole record to touch one field. Hold that thought.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:09 – 0:12

Why we ever denormalized: the economics flipped

Normalization is a storage-saving technique from an era when storage was the expensive resource. That era ended. But the reflexes it created — and the reflexes the next era created — are still driving models.

Then: storage dear, joins cheap enough

Disk cost real money, so you stored each fact once and reassembled it with joins. Codd's normal forms are, at bottom, a deduplication discipline.

Now: storage cheap, compute & latency dear

Storage is nearly free; the scarce resources are CPU cycles, network hops, and human milliseconds. So you pre-shape data for the read — denormalize at read time.

The move the industry missed

The right response to cheap storage isn't "throw away the normalized truth." It's "keep the normalized truth, and pay cheap storage to also materialize the shapes each consumer wants." Denormalization becomes a projection, not a fork. You get read-time shape without giving up write-time correctness — because the engine maintains both.

Denormalization was always about spending the cheap resource to save the dear one. The mistake was making it a one-way, hand-maintained copy instead of an engine-maintained projection.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:12 – 0:15

Codd already drew the map

Normalized relational is the canonical form — the minimal, non-redundant statement of the facts. Everything else a consumer wants is a view over it. Codd's own relational algebra defined the projections; we just stopped materializing them where they hurt.

Model the domain in canonical form. Project the access. The document is a projection. The graph is a projection. The vector index is a projection. One truth, many shapes.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:15 – 0:18

Polyglot persistence: five stores, five versions of the truth

"Purpose-built" sounds disciplined. In practice it means the same fact lives in five engines with five consistency models — and nothing owns the reconciliation. That was tolerable when a human read the answer. It is not tolerable when an AI acts on it.

The integration tax

Every cross-store request pays serialization, a network hop, and an auth handshake. Chains of them dominate latency and become the thing you spend your quarter debugging.

The consistency gap

The document store, the search index, and the vector store converge eventually. Between writes they disagree. Which copy did the model just read?

The AI failure mode

Feed a model stale + conflicting facts and it doesn't error — it confidently hallucinates. When it then takes an action, the stale context becomes a business liability.

State Vector Dissonance: the vector index reflects the world as of the last embed job; the operational tables reflect the world as of this transaction. RAG retrieves from the first and reasons as if it were the second. The wider the gap, the more confident the wrong answer.

AI didn't create this flaw — it removed our tolerance for it. Immediate consistency across vectors, documents, graph, and relational stops being a nicety and becomes a correctness requirement.

Unified Model Theory: the vocabulary for the rest of the day

UMT is just Codd's insight, made operational and given names. Five terms — once you have them, every modeling decision in this deck has a precise place to live.

TermWhat it isIn practice

Projected ShapeThe form a consumer wants to readA document, a graph, a vector, a flat rowset
ProjectionThe declarative mapping between themA duality view, a materialized view, an index

Model once. Project as documents, graphs, time series, or relations. Serve every consumer from one source of truth. That sentence is the whole theory — the rest is physics and tradeoffs.

Act 1 · Why converged, why now0:20 – 0:22

One query, four models, one transaction

The theory only earns its keep if the engine can serve every projection together. Here relational, JSON, graph, and vector answer a single question — no hops, no copies, no eventual anything.

Find the 5 parties nearest a query embedding, tier from their JSON profile, reachable through ≤3 control hops

SELECT p.name,
       JSON_VALUE(p.profile, '$.tier')                -- JSON projection
       VECTOR_DISTANCE(p.embedding, :q, COSINE) AS score  -- vector
FROM   parties p
WHERE  p.party_id IN (
         SELECT owner FROM GRAPH_TABLE ( ownership          -- graph
           MATCH (a) -[:CONTROLS]->{1,3} (b)
           WHERE a.party_id = :root
           COLUMNS (b.party_id AS owner) ) )
ORDER BY score
FETCH FIRST 5 ROWS ONLY;                            -- relational

Four access dimensions, one statement, one consistency model, one security boundary. This is the payoff of "model once, project many" — and it's the standard every pattern in the next hour is measured against.

Act 2 · The physics0:22 – 0:26

Three knobs decide every model

Strip away the vocabulary and every modeling decision — embed or reference, precompute or query — turns on three measurable quantities. Set these, and the model is half-chosen for you.

Diversity of access · read/write ratio · update % and where it lands. High diversity wants projections. Read-heavy wants precomputation. But knob 3 can veto both — which is where the patterns come from.

Act 2 · The physics0:26 – 0:29

Why the patterns exist: write amplification

Every document pattern in the catalog is, underneath, a reaction to one physical fact: in a document engine, touching one field can rewrite the whole record. Understand this and the patterns stop being folklore.

The bigger and hotter the document, the more every write costs. Bucket, Computed, Subset, Pre-Allocation — they all exist to keep documents small and writes cheap. They are write-amplification management, dressed up as design patterns.

Act 2 · The physics0:29 – 0:31

The breakpoint: when precomputing stops paying

Every "should I maintain this precomputed shape?" decision reduces to one inequality. Precompute only when the reads you save outweigh the writes you add.

What moves the right side up: more writes, fatter documents, worse write amplification — a subset trimmed on every write to serve a read that rarely happens.

What a cost-based optimizer does: it lowers read_cost — an index-only Top-N, a hash join, parallel scan — so the inequality holds at a shape you never had to hand-maintain.

A better optimizer moves the breakpoint. Patterns that "must" be precomputed on a document engine can be queried live on a converged one — because the read side just got cheaper.

Act 2 · The physics0:31 – 0:33

Duality doesn't change the knobs — it changes how they get set

The document catalog offers two resolutions to every modeling tension: embed or reference. Converged adds a third the catalog never had — project it — and it's the one that dissolves the tradeoff instead of trading one cost for another.

Resolution 1

Embed

Fast reads, but you copy the fact. Every copy is write amplification and an update anomaly waiting to happen. Great until knob 3 turns.

Resolution 2

Reference

No duplication, but the read now needs $lookup or a second round trip — and the document engine has no optimizer to make that join cheap.

Resolution 3 — new

Project it

Store the fact once in canonical form; let a duality view project it into the document shape, live, maintained by the engine. Read shape without write penalty.

Same three knobs. But now embed/reference isn't a permanent architectural fork — it's a projection choice you can change without a migration. The needle you couldn't move before is now a setting.

Act 3 · Pattern 1 — Extended Reference0:35 – 0:40

Wealth management: the advisor who moved offices

To avoid a lookup, the advisor's contact card is embedded in every client and every account document. Reads are instant. Then the advisor changes offices.

Document — fan-out update across hundreds of thousands of copies

db.clients.updateMany(
  { "advisor.id": "A-317" },
  { $set: { "advisor.office": "Leeds" } }
)
// same again on accounts, statements,
// alerts ... every collection that
// embedded the card. Miss one =
// an update anomaly the LLM will read.

Converged — project the mutable, copy only the immutable

CREATE JSON RELATIONAL DUALITY VIEW client_dv AS
  Clients @insert @update @delete {
    _id      : client_id
    name     : name
    advisor  : Advisors @noinsert @nodelete {   -- ref, live
                 id     : advisor_id
                 office : office
               }
  };
-- advisor moves once, in one row; every
-- projected client doc reflects it instantly.

The needle flips the moment the "reference" field can change. Snapshot the immutable (the trade price at execution); project the mutable (the advisor's current office). One fact, one place, zero fan-out.

Act 3 · Pattern 2 — Computed0:40 – 0:45

Telecom: the cycle-usage counter on a hot document

Each subscriber doc carries a running cycle-usage rollup so the app never re-aggregates. But every call detail record now re-writes the parent document — a write storm on the hottest docs you own.

Document — counter-sharding to survive the write storm

// every CDR rewrites the subscriber doc;
// to spread the heat you split the counter
// into N shard-docs, then fan-in on read:
db.usage_shards.aggregate([
  { $match: { sub: "S-9" } },
  { $group: { _id: "$sub",
              bytes: { $sum: "$bytes" } } }
])  // wrote once, now read N docs to total it

Converged — the rollup with staleness = 0

CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW mv_cycle_usage
  REFRESH FAST ON COMMIT AS          -- engine maintains it
SELECT subscriber_id,
       SUM(bytes) AS cycle_bytes,
       COUNT(*)    AS cdr_count
FROM   cdr
GROUP BY subscriber_id;
-- "Computed", but transactional and current.

Value-add — the hot Top-N: "top 20 subscribers by usage this cycle" is agony in a document engine (re-sort millions, or maintain a leaderboard doc). Converged: a composite index on (cycle_bytes DESC, subscriber_id) makes it an index-only scan — 60s → 500ms, fully ACID, nothing to maintain.

The needle flips when write frequency on the parent exceeds the reads the rollup saves. Move the aggregation into an engine-maintained projection and the write storm disappears — the counter is current without touching a hot document.

Act 3 · Pattern 3 — Bucket0:45 – 0:50

Manufacturing IoT: the bucket that marches to 16 MB

Sensor readings are bucketed per machine, per hour to keep document counts down. It's the canonical write-amplification pattern: every reading $push-es into the bucket and re-serializes it — and hot sensors march the bucket toward the hard cap.

Document — the bucket grows, the write cost grows with it

db.readings.updateOne(
  { machine: "M-118", hr: "2026-09-25T14" },
  { $push: { pts: { t: ts, v: 71.4 } },
    $inc:  { n: 1 } }
)
// each push re-serializes the whole bucket;
// a busy sensor hits the 16 MB doc cap and
// analytics can't scale past one bucket.

Converged — partitioned relational time-series

-- readings: RANGE-partitioned on ts, auto-interval
SELECT /*+ PARALLEL(8) */ machine_id,
       TRUNC(ts,'HH') AS hour,
       AVG(val), MAX(val), COUNT(*)
FROM   readings
WHERE  ts >= SYSTIMESTAMP - INTERVAL '7' DAY
GROUP BY machine_id, TRUNC(ts,'HH');
-- append-only inserts; no cap, no re-serialize.

Value-add — $sql in the pipeline: a document team keeps its aggregation pipeline and drops full SQL — parallel scan, window functions, joins — into one $sql stage. The heavy analytic that timed out as a pipeline runs to completion with no 16 MB / 100 MB pipeline caps. Scale-out parallelism, mid-pipeline.

The needle flips on the hottest sensors — exactly the ones you care about. Append-only inserts have flat write cost; the rollup is a GROUP BY over a partition range, parallelized by the optimizer, not by you.

Act 3 · Pattern 4 — Subset0:50 – 0:55

Insurance: the recent-claims subset nobody reads

A policy document keeps the 10 most recent claims inline for a fast summary; cold history lives elsewhere. Claims arrive constantly, so you push-and-trim on every write — to serve a full read that happens rarely.

Document — maintain the subset on every write

db.policies.updateOne(
  { _id: "P-42" },
  { $push: { recentClaims: {
      $each:  [ newClaim ],
      $sort:  { ts: -1 },
      $slice: 10 } } }   // trim to 10
)
// write amplification on the write path to
// optimize a read path that's mostly idle.

Converged — stop maintaining it; query it

-- composite index: (policy_id, event_ts DESC)
SELECT claim_id, amount, event_ts
FROM   claims
WHERE  policy_id = :id
ORDER BY event_ts DESC
FETCH FIRST 10 ROWS ONLY;
-- index-only, O(log n) seek + 10-row scan.
-- a duality view reunites hot + cold on read.

Subset is a bet that reads dwarf writes. When they don't, you're paying the write side to optimize a read that hardly fires. A pre-sorted composite index gives the same instant Top-N with zero write-path cost.

Act 3 · Pattern 5 — Tree / Hierarchy0:55 – 1:00

Manufacturing: the bill of materials you have to re-parent

A BOM stored as a materialized path makes "everything under this assembly" a fast prefix scan. Then engineering re-parents a subassembly — and you rewrite the path of every descendant. And "which assemblies use part X?" a prefix can't answer at all.

Document — re-parent rewrites the whole subtree

// move subassembly SA-7 under A-2:
db.bom.updateMany(
  { path: /^root\.A-1\.SA-7/ },
  [ { $set: { path: { $replaceOne: {
        input: "$path",
        find:  "root.A-1.SA-7",
        replacement: "root.A-2.SA-7" } } } } ]
)  // write amp ∝ size of the subtree

Converged — trees stay cheap, graphs become possible

-- explosion: every descendant of an assembly
SELECT part_id, LEVEL depth FROM bom
START WITH parent_id = :asm
CONNECT BY PRIOR part_id = parent_id;

-- reverse, N-hops: which assemblies use a part?
SELECT * FROM GRAPH_TABLE ( bom_graph
  MATCH (p) <-[:USED_IN]-{1,} (a)
  WHERE p.part_id = :part
  COLUMNS (a.part_id) );
-- re-parent = ONE edge update.

A path encodes one traversal direction and pays to maintain it. Split the concern: let the tree be edges (re-parent is a single row), and let genuine graph questions be graph queries — the prefix trick never could express them.

Act 3 · Pattern 6 — Outlier1:00 – 1:05

Financial: the mega-advisor who breaks the model

99% of advisors have a few hundred clients and embed fine. Then there's the institutional book with 100,000 clients. The Outlier pattern adds a flag, an overflow collection, and an if in your application — the 16 MB limit leaking into your code.

Document — the model admits it breaks for the fat tail

const a = db.advisors.findOne({_id: id})
let clients = a.clients
if (a.hasOverflow) {                 // the leak
  clients = clients.concat(
    db.overflow.find({advisor: id})
              .toArray())
}
// two code paths, two failure modes,
// forever — because one advisor is big.

Converged — the outlier is just more rows

-- advisor_clients partitioned/indexed by advisor
SELECT client_id, market_value
FROM   advisor_clients
WHERE  advisor_id = :whale
ORDER BY market_value DESC
FETCH FIRST 100 ROWS ONLY;
-- CBO adapts the plan for the whale vs the
-- typical advisor. One query. No branch.

There is no fat tail when the row is the unit, not the document. Embed the bounded common case, reference the overflow, and let a duality view re-join them — but on a relational base the outlier never needed special-casing at all. The optimizer plans for it.

Act 3 · The rest of the catalog1:05 – 1:09

The rest of the catalog, at speed

The remaining patterns split cleanly into two piles: those that survive as optional conveniences, and those that become obsolete the moment the engine changes.

Survive — now optional

Polymorphic — heterogeneous rows coexist; a multivalue index spans them. Attribute — sparse key/value arrays get a real index instead of a scan. Schema-Versioning — IS JSON + a discriminator; a view papers over versions. Document-Versioning → temporal validity / Flashback gives you history for free.

Obsolete — artifacts of a limit

Approximation — hand-rolled counters replaced by native APPROX_COUNT_DISTINCT. Pre-Allocation — padding a document so it won't move on disk was an MMAPv1 artifact; it vanished the moment the storage engine changed. The cleanest proof that patterns are bets against an engine, not laws of modeling.

Pre-Allocation is the tell. Nobody decided it was wrong — the engine changed and it evaporated. That's every pattern's eventual fate when the constraint it fought is lifted.

Act 3 · The honest edge1:09 – 1:13

Where the document model still wins

This is not "relational beats document." If it were a pitch, I'd skip this slide. Some workloads are genuinely document-shaped — and forcing a duality view on them would be the same mistake in the other direction.

The white-hot shopping cart

Tiny, self-contained, written constantly, read by exactly one owner, no cross-entity query. Model it as a single collection keyed cart#sku — small inserts and deletes, no write amplification, no join anyone needs.

Do not wrap it in a duality view. There's no canonical form fighting to get out.

The rule

Reach for a projection when a fact is shared, queried many ways, or changes under you. Leave it embedded when it's private, uniform, and self-contained.

Duality is a tool, not a hammer. A single collection is not a duality view.

The credibility of everything in this deck rests on this slide. The workload chooses the shape — and sometimes it chooses a plain document. The point was never one model; it was refusing to pay for a model the workload didn't ask for.

Act 4 · How you know where the needle flips1:13 – 1:17

Don't argue the model — bake it off

Every slide so far named a breakpoint. How do you find yours? You don't reason your way to it — you reason your way to the candidates, then measure. This is the oracle-ai-dev method.

Two rules that catch every bad instinct:  ① Writes are access patterns too — a shape that wins reads and loses writes hasn't won.  ② A win at the wrong scale is not a win — measure at production cardinality or you're tuning a toy.

Reason to the candidates; measure to the decision. The bake-off is how "I think we should embed this" becomes "at 40M rows and this write rate, D3 wins by 6×, and here's the crossover."

Act 4 · How you know where the needle flips1:20 – 1:23

And sometimes the document shape wins — on the same engine

Measurement cuts both ways. The honest result isn't "relational always wins" — it's "shape per pattern, one engine." Here's the case that proves the method isn't rigged.

Entitlements: array-containment

"Which resources can this principal touch?" is an array-containment query. Modeled across a 27-table normalized schema it's a join thicket. Modeled as a document with an indexed array, on the same converged engine, it ran ~9–15× faster.

The 90/10, honestly

~90% of queries are happy on a document shape; ~10% desperately need joins, aggregation, or graph. The win isn't picking a side — it's serving both shapes from one source of truth, each query on the shape that suits it.

The converged argument is not "always relational." It's that one engine can hold the canonical form and project whatever shape each access pattern wants — and the measurement, not the meeting, decides which.

Act 5 · Close1:23 – 1:26

What "converged" has to mean

"Multi-model" is a marketing word — most products meaning several engines behind one logo. Converged is a physical claim. Five tests separate the two:

One transaction boundary

A single ACID commit spans document, relational, graph, and vector — not a saga across services.

One optimizer

A cost-based optimizer that plans across models, not a separate planner per store.

One consistency model

No eventual gap between the vector index and the row it describes.

One governance domain

One auth model, one audit trail, one patch cycle, one attack surface.

Shared access surfaces

SQL, SQL/JSON, SQL/PGQ — and the Mongo wire protocol — over the same rows.

The test

If any answer is "that's a different service," it's polyglot with a shared bill — not converged.

Every softening in this deck depended on these five holding at once. Remove any one and you're back to integration tax and consistency gaps — the problems the patterns were coping with in the first place.

Act 5 · Close1:26 – 1:28

Five questions for any architecture — including yours

Take these back to your own systems. They work on any stack, any vendor. If you can't answer them, that's the finding.

  1. Where is your canonical form? If the answer is "in five places," you have no canonical form — you have five copies negotiating.
  2. What projected shapes do your consumers actually need? Document, graph, vector, flat — list them per consumer.
  3. What access dimensions? Point read, range, aggregate, traverse, similarity — which ones, how often?
  4. What's your projection latency? Synchronous = zero lag. Async CDC = seconds to minutes of disagreement. Which can your use case tolerate?
  5. What consistency does your AI need? Eventual consistency is a hallucination-risk setting. Is that the setting you meant to choose?

Question 4 is the quiet one. Most "converged" claims fail on projection latency — the shapes exist, but they're minutes stale. Sync projection is the difference between a view and a copy.
